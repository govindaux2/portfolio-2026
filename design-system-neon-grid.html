What the neon grid is actually made of

There is no 3D model, no mesh and no camera here. Every pixel independently works out "what would I be looking at if I were part of a floor stretching away from the viewer?" and colours itself. Eight layers are added on top of each other, in this order. Turn each one off in the Layers section to see exactly what it contributes.

1 · The sky backdrop

A vertical gradient, darkest at the top, warming as it approaches the horizon line. It's the cheapest layer and it does most of the mood work — everything else is drawn on top of it.

Controls: Horizon height moves the split point. Everything else in the scene is measured from this line, so it's the single most powerful slider on the page.

2 · The sun focal point

A circle, drawn only when the pixel's distance from a centre point is less than the radius. The horizontal slices are one line of maths: keep the pixel only if sin(height × sliceCount) is positive, which cuts regular gaps. The glow around it is distance falling off exponentially.

Controls: Show sun, Sun size, Sun height, Slice count. It follows your pointer horizontally, which is why the scene feels like it's looking back at you.

3 · The perspective floor the trick

This is the only clever part. For any pixel below the horizon, measure how far below it is — call that depth. Then the distance of that point on the floor from the camera is roughly 1 / depth. Pixels just under the horizon have a tiny depth, so they map to enormous distances; pixels at the bottom of the screen are right at your feet.

That single division is what creates perspective. It's also why the grid squashes together near the horizon without any 3D maths at all.

Controls: Cell width (across) and Cell depth (toward you) set the grid spacing — these are separate on purpose, because a floor that's wide and shallow reads very differently from a square one. Line thickness and Line brightness do what they say.

4 · The scroll motion

Add time to the depth coordinate before drawing the lines, and the whole floor slides toward you. Only the coordinate moves — the lines themselves are recomputed every frame from scratch.

Control: Scroll speed. Set it to zero and the floor freezes but the sweep keeps running, which is a good way to see that they're independent.

5 · The sweep your favourite

This is the pulse travelling toward you. Take the distance-from-camera value, add time, take the fractional part, and you get a sawtooth that repeats every so many metres and slides forward as time passes. Feed that through exp(-x / width) and instead of a hard band you get a bright leading edge with a soft tail behind it — which is what makes it read as a wave rather than a stripe.

Crucially the sweep does two things at once: it lifts the whole floor slightly, and it supercharges any grid line it's currently passing over. That combination is why it looks like energy moving through the surface rather than a light shining on it.

Controls: Sweep rate (how fast toward you), Sweep spacing (how many at once), Sweep length (short sharp pulse vs long comet tail), Sweep intensity. Clicking the floor or pressing space launches an extra single pulse on top.

5b · Bending the horizon one number

The flat version measures depth as "how far below the horizon line am I". The curved version measures "how far inside a circle am I". Those are the same calculation — a straight line is just a circle with an enormous radius. So Horizon curve does nothing more than shrink one radius, from about 60 screen-heights down to under half of one.

Everything else reorganises itself for free, which is the nice part. The crossbars were already drawn at fixed distances from the viewer, so once distance is measured from a centre point they become concentric rings. The vanishing lines were drawn at fixed angles, so they become spokes. And the sweep was already a function of distance, so it stops being a band rolling in from the horizon and becomes a ring pulsing out from the middle — which is the look you were after.

Two ways to get rings, worth knowing the difference: Horizon curve bends the world itself, so the whole scene becomes a planet. Sweep origin leaves the world flat and only moves where the sweep is measured from, so you keep a normal perspective floor with ripples spreading across it from a point. Try the Ripple preset for the second and Planet for the first.

5c · Changing the view camera

There's no real camera, but four sliders do the same job. Camera height is the number that controls how fast perspective compresses — low values feel like lying on the floor, high values like standing and looking down. Zoom scales the whole coordinate system, which is how you frame a planet. Roll rotates it. Ceiling mirror reuses the ground calculation for pixels above the horizon, turning a floor into a tunnel — that's the Tunnel preset.

6 · Fog depth cue

Brightness multiplied by exp(-distance × amount), so far things dim out. Without it the grid turns into a solid sheet of noise at the horizon, because infinitely many lines fall into one pixel. Fog is doing an anti-aliasing job as much as an atmospheric one.

Control: Draw distance. Push it high and watch the horizon turn into mush — that's the artefact fog exists to hide.

7 · Horizon haze the glue

A tight band of accent colour along the horizon line. Small detail, disproportionate effect: it makes the sky and the floor feel like the same world rather than two images stacked.

8 · Film pass finishing

Scanlines (a sine wave over screen height), vignette (darkening with distance from centre), grain (random noise), and finally a tone-map: colour / (1 + colour). That last one matters — without it every neon highlight clips to flat white and the whole thing looks cheap. It's the shader equivalent of not blowing out your highlights.

How to modify it as a designer

  • Change the mood first with Horizon height and Draw distance. Low horizon plus short draw distance feels intimate; high horizon plus long distance feels epic.
  • Grid density is a typography decision. Sparse reads premium, dense reads technical.
  • The sweep should be slower than you think. Anything above about 1.2 starts to feel anxious.
  • Two colours maximum. The accent carries the grid, the secondary carries the sun and haze.
