Manufacturing intelligence, told as it happened.

I was design lead, with a team of five. QAD wanted a tool that catches problems early, helps people work together, and protects margin. This follows the work from mid-June through the September events, and what customers asked for after.

Decisions on the floor were taking weeks.

The cause was hard to see, so the line waited. People were retiring, and the judgment they carried was leaving with them. Customers were also staying on older ERP systems. The product had to be a reason to move, and a way to use AI in a way that fits a plant.

Expertise was leaving

As people retired, decisions that used to live with them were getting lost.

The cause was unclear

Without a root cause, or a way to see the impact, a decision on the floor could take weeks.

Margin was eroding

A late part became a broken promise. Catching that earlier was the point.

Older systems stayed

Customers needed a reason to leave the ERP they already had, or to choose QAD over another one.

AIRA was already in the market.

AIRA is the competitor. It leans on AI, and we took inspiration from it. We were not trying to copy it. QAD’s systems are larger, so the reason to build our own was to keep that breadth and stay closer to how a plant actually runs.

The work fell into three phases.

I have written it in the order things happened. Phase 1 started in mid-June. Phase 2 started in mid-July, and the handoff to development started in the first week of August. Phase 3 is what we heard from customers after the events.

Phase 1

Learn the domain, agree what the first version covers, and put something real in front of engineering and leadership.

Phase 2

Build that version for Champions of Manufacturing in Chicago and Munich, so customers could try it.

Phase 3

Talk to the people who signed up. They changed what should come first.

We learned the plant before drawing screens.

Workshops ran in person and online, with the QAD team onshore and offshore. The first cut was the problems people already had, not a feature list. The business broke into three stages: source to pay, plan to produce, and order to cash. Each stage had more roles than we could design for.

We cut the scope to one case.

With the product managers, we narrowed the first version to supply delay. The people it had to serve were a buyer, a production planner, a VP, and customer service. Other cases, including OTIF protection, stayed out of this release. Three parts carried it: a glossary, dashboards, and an assistant that only answered what.

The glossary came from the ERP they already run.

We did not invent the metrics. We took the KPIs and formulas already in QAD’s ERP, then sat with the functional product managers and asked what each one means, who uses it, and how it helps them decide. That became one living document, and the glossary was written from it. It also had to work for customers who are not on QAD, and for customers who stay on an older version. The layer expands to their system.

Picture · the KPI document

Dashboards started from something already built.

Floor managers had been asking engineers for every dashboard change, and each change took weeks. The first version did not open on a blank canvas. People got a set of prebuilt dashboards, so they could see the data and play with it. After that they could build one themselves, or ask the assistant to build one. Underneath is Superset, with our interface on top. Cube was the other option. We kept Superset.

Picture · a prebuilt dashboard, then someone changing it

The assistant only answered what.

For the first version we left out why, and we left out root cause. A number is easier to trust when you can see where it came from. A useful answer was a count, a chart, a table, or a dashboard made in the moment. This is a small part of the app, about a fifth of it, and it is what people open most. Why, root cause, and a recommendation were planned for a later release in February.

Picture · a question, the chart that comes back, and where the number came from

We showed the work before we locked it.

In Mumbai we had about three days to put up a prototype engineering could react to. The first prototypes were in React, so we could get them in front of people on time. We later moved to Angular, because that is what QAD already runs. In Bangalore, leadership sat with the mocks and we kept changing them in the room. A small design system, built in about two days, kept the team drawing in one language. Phase 1 ended with the scope shown. The build had not started.

Phase 2 was the build for September.

From mid-July we built the version customers could try at Champions of Manufacturing in Chicago and Munich. The handoff to development started in the first week of August. The interface also had to work in German. That is noted here, and not expanded yet.

The design system for this phase is the Figma file, QAD Design System V3. Color, type, spacing, icons, and the component pages live there. I have not pulled frames into this page yet.

Open the React demo. This is the supplier-delay prototype, reused as it was built. The later Angular app is QAD Design Internal. It is not built into a page here yet, so it is not linked as something you can click through.

Two tracks ran at once. One was the product for the event. The other was the agentic work: by February the assistant should answer why, find a root cause, and suggest what to do next. We ran workshops with about sixteen functional product managers and found roughly fifteen or sixteen decision loops. We could not map how a decision is carried out in real time, so this track closed on assumptions. For the event we cut the story to supply delay, and those loops down to four. The four titles are still to be named.

Identified decisions

The event worked.

More than thirty customers signed up in the first two days. The CEO, Sanjay Brahmavarm, showed the prototype on the center stage. NVIDIA signed up to help. What people responded to was simple: the daily work could move onto the system, so a person spends the time on the decision.

After a signup, a customer tries the demo for about two weeks, then brings their own data. A full setup is about four months. The first setup, the canonical layer and queries that return properly, was expected to take two to three weeks. As of now, about seventy large plants have signed up for bring-your-own-data.

Customers did not want supply delay first.

In the interviews they asked for the ordinary work: updating purchase orders, and replying to suppliers and to the other people in the loop. Supply delay could come after that. The roles for that first layer are the production planner, the buyer, the VP, sales, and the sales executive. We are not building it from nothing. Kavida is already in use, with persona agents that already have skills. The later call was to put our interface on those agents.

From September we designed the end state first, sat with engineering, and toned it down. The same stretch covered the foundation: user management, tenant management, auditing, and governance. Next is another cut with engineering, then a check with customers on what February actually needs.

What I take from it.

The scope kept moving as we understood the plant. The first pass had to stay a draft. Design, product, frontend, and leadership had to get back to one picture when the mock, the Figma file, and the Angular build drifted apart. The early work was in Figma. Later it was Figma and Claude, in a faster loop. Pieces that would have taken months were done in weeks.

One hard product call was speed. An answer was taking about 20 seconds, and the bar we set was 4 seconds. We moved to AG-UI so the screen shows which tools are in use, instead of waiting on a blank page. The name of the earlier, slower path is still to be confirmed.
